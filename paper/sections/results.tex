\subsection{Forced-choice probe: clones win, but why?}
\label{sec:res-probe}
The forced-choice probe confronts each agent with one gold product and one
synthetic listing (300 randomized duels). Agents overall still prefer the
real product, $\sigma=0.377$ (95\% CI $[0.324,0.433]$, binomial vs.\
$\tfrac12$ $p=2.3\times10^{-5}$), but the families separate sharply:
$\sigma_{\text{clone}}=0.523$ --- statistically indistinguishable from
chance --- while $\sigma_{\text{geo}}=0.351$ and
$\sigma_{\text{reviews}}=0.287$. Taken alone, this pattern invites a
perceptual reading: specification clones seem to pass as real products.
Trajectory inspection is consistent with such a reading --- agents quote
instruction attributes and buy the real product even when it displays no
rating --- but trajectories cannot separate what the agent \emph{fails to
see} from what it \emph{prefers to buy}. The next subsection closes that
gap with controlled channel ablations, and reaches the opposite conclusion.

\subsection{Channel decomposition: preference, not perception}
\label{sec:res-mechanism}
We re-run the clone duels under three controlled ablations of the synthetic
listing: \textbf{price parity} (clone priced exactly at the gold price),
\textbf{rating parity} (rating and review count reset to the gold listing's
genuine values), and \textbf{both} (300 duels each, same tasks and pair
seeds). Table~\ref{tab:mech} gives the result. Either channel alone drops
the clone win-rate from $0.523$ to $0.386$ (price parity: paired McNemar
$+9/-21$, $p=0.043$; rating parity: $+5/-17$, $p=0.017$); removing both
drops it to $0.250$ (95\% CI $[0.171,0.350]$; paired $+3/-27$,
$p<10^{-4}$). The decomposition is additive to within half a point:
$0.523-0.137$ (price) $-0.136$ (social proof) $=0.250$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a).
Two conclusions
follow. First, the clone's advantage is \emph{bought} with a discount and
manufactured ratings, in equal shares --- not earned by perceptual
similarity: at full price and honest ratings the clone is rejected almost
twice as often as chosen. Second, the residual $0.250$ lies \emph{below}
the plain-gloss baseline $\sigma_{\text{geo}}=0.351$: once the economic
lures are removed, a listing that near-copies the gold product's
specifications is a \emph{liability} for the attacker, plausibly because
the close-but-altered specification text makes the mismatch easier to
notice.

\paragraph{A threshold law for discount pricing.}
Because price is the dominant channel, we dose it directly: the clone is
priced at a fixed fraction $f\in\{1.0,0.9,0.8,0.7\}$ of the gold price
with ratings left inflated (300 duels per level; paired against the
$f=1.0$ arm). The response is a \emph{threshold}, not a gradient: at price
parity agents reject the clone ($\sigma=0.386$), at $-10\%$ they already
prefer it ($\sigma=0.591$; paired $+26/-8$, $p=0.003$), and deeper
discounts add nothing ($0.534$ at $-20\%$, $p=0.015$; $0.568$ at $-30\%$,
$p=0.004$) --- all discount levels sit within noise of the original
mixed-discount pool ($0.523$). Figure~\ref{fig:mech}b plots the response.
In deployment terms, \emph{any} meaningful discount suffices for the
attack; the agent's price sensitivity saturates immediately past the
threshold (the deeper-discount points are statistically indistinguishable
from the $10\%$ point: two-proportion $p\geq0.45$).

\subsection{Dose--response: regret outruns success}
\label{sec:res-dose}
Figure~\ref{fig:dose} and Table~\ref{tab:dose} give the main curve over the
effective dose grid $\varepsilon\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75\}$ (with $N{=}10$
candidates, the nominal $0.1$ dose maps to zero replaced slots and is
omitted; each dose corresponds to 0, 2, 4, or 6 replaced slots),
$N{=}100$ tasks per dose under the Qwen2.5-7B backbone, plus a full
second-seed replication of the endpoints ($N{=}200$ paired observations).
Three co-movements define the silent-erosion signature.
\textbf{Success falls at the first dose step and stays depressed}: from $0.590$ at $\varepsilon=0$ to
$0.360$ at $\varepsilon=0.75$ (McNemar on paired tasks: 28 vs.\ 5
discordant, $\chi^2=14.7$, $p=1.3\times10^{-4}$; pooling both seeds, 64
vs.\ 13, $\chi^2=32.5$, $p=1.2\times10^{-8}$).
\textbf{Regret climbs even faster}: mean regret $0.262\to0.443$ ($+69\%$),
paired endpoint difference $+0.181$ (bootstrap 95\% CI $[+0.095,+0.264]$,
$t=4.2$). \textbf{Confidence rises throughout}: post-hoc verbalized
confidence increases from $0.822$ to $0.875$ while expected calibration
error \emph{doubles} ($0.249\to0.496$) --- agents err more while trusting
themselves more. The intermediate non-monotonicity of the curve (regret
$0.429\to0.401$ from $\varepsilon{=}0.25$ to $0.5$) is within paired
task-level noise: the paired regret difference has bootstrap 95\% CI
$[-0.102,+0.044]$, and the small success rebound is likewise
indistinguishable from zero ($+0.070$, CI $[-0.020,+0.160]$).
The purchase share tells the mechanism directly: synthetic
listings are bought in 53\%, 42\%, and 54\% of tasks at
$\varepsilon=0.25,0.5,0.75$ respectively (from 0\% at $\varepsilon=0$;
Cochran--Armitage trend $z=6.98$, $p=1.4\times10^{-12}$), and purchases of
synthetic listings carry mean reward $0.35$ versus $0.80$ for purchases of
real listings at the same dose (both contamination seeds pooled).

\subsection{Family attribution}
\label{sec:res-family}
Single-family runs at $\varepsilon=0.75$ (Table~\ref{tab:family}) attribute
the damage. Clone contamination is the most destructive at the success
level (success $0.600\to0.425$, $-17.5$\,pp; regret $+41\%$; synthetic
listings bought in $52.5\%$ of tasks), consistent with the probe's
decomposition --- clones carry the two lures the agent is most sensitive
to. Review-inflated pages deliver the largest regret
shock ($0.294\to0.454$, $+54\%$): a plain page with a manufactured five-star
flood gets purchased ($37.5\%$ of tasks) precisely because it looks
credible, then fails on missing specifications. Geo contamination is the
mildest --- success flat, regret $+11.8\%$ at seed 1 --- but we flag the
attribution honestly: a full second-seed replication of the geo family
gives a pooled paired regret difference of $+0.075$ whose 95\% CI
$[-0.050,+0.195]$ crosses zero, so we treat ``quiet attrition'' for this
family as \emph{directional} evidence only. Two facts about geo
contamination are nevertheless solid: it does not measurably change task
success, and when a geo page \emph{is} purchased it earns a mean reward of
only $0.22$ (versus $0.985$ for gold purchases in the same arm) --- the
rare geo purchase is a near-total loss.

\subsection{Defenses: provenance beats detection}
\label{sec:res-defense}
Table~\ref{tab:defense} evaluates the defense battery at
$\varepsilon=0.75$ against the paired uncontaminated baseline (same tasks,
same seeds; the \texttt{none} row is the unprotected high-dose condition).
Provenance re-ranking with an agent-visible \texttt{[verified]} badge ---
computed from a \emph{noisy} provenance bit (85\% coverage on real items,
15\% spurious on synthetic ones) --- recovers the full regret excess
(0.398$\to$0.329, versus the uncontaminated 0.338) and cuts synthetic
purchases from 47.5\% to 32.5\%; the oracle badge (perfect provenance)
restores success \emph{above} the uncontaminated level (0.750 vs.\ 0.525)
and pushes regret below it (0.171), because knowing which listings are
verified makes agents scrutinize specifications harder. The structural
slop filter (marketing-cue density, keyword stuffing, bullet symmetry)
also fully recovers (regret 0.250, success 0.575): the generator families
leak templated structure even when their text is organic. Detection-style
defenses, by contrast, are brittle in deployment: the perplexity filter,
calibrated to near-perfect separation on held-out listings, collapses at
high dose (success 0.200, \emph{below} the unprotected 0.450) because its
hard threshold drops the true product --- whose generic text looks
``predictable'' --- while a tail of synthetic pages survives. The matched
random filter confirms the mechanism: dropping 30\% of candidates uniformly
\emph{raises} the clean baseline (regret 0.172) yet does nothing against
contamination (0.431) --- filtering \emph{amount} is not filtering
\emph{quality}. The prompt defense achieves an intermediate 57\% regret
recovery but changes behavior on the clean Web as well (a ``skepticism
tax''), so its net value is smaller than its high-dose delta suggests.
Beyond the battery, four targeted experiments pin down \emph{how}
provenance information should be consumed and how robust the defenses are
(all at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$, 40 paired tasks, seed-1 pools unless noted).

\textbf{(i) Order, don't score.} Re-implementing the provenance bit as a
soft bonus $\lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}[\text{verified}]$ added to the platform
score fails for every weight within the rating scale --- regret $0.507$
($\lambda{=}0.5$), $0.444$ ($1$), $0.494$ ($2$), $0.464$ ($4$), $0.360$
($6$) --- and only recovers at $\lambda{=}8$ ($0.332$, statistically tied
with the hard re-ranker's $0.329$;
Figure~\ref{fig:stress}a). A bonus smaller than the typical gap
between a manufactured 4.9-star rating and a genuine one is simply outbid:
provenance must act as a \emph{lexicographic} ordering constraint, not an
additive score.

\textbf{(ii) Forgery envelope.} Letting the adversary forge the badge on
$s\%$ of synthetic listings leaves recovery unchanged across the envelope:
regret $0.313$ ($s{=}35$), $0.332$ ($50$), $0.325$ ($65$) --- all
statistically indistinguishable from the no-forgery $0.329$ (Wilcoxon
$p\geq0.96$; Figure~\ref{fig:stress}b). The lone $s{=}25\%$ point scores
\emph{better} than baseline ($0.222$, $\Delta=-0.107$, $p=0.012$); five
trajectory replays attribute
the gap to greedy-chain behavioral variance rather than a mechanism, and
one favorable outlier among four tested rates is consistent with
multiplicity. The mechanism is consistent with the rest of our measurements:
recovery is driven by the \emph{ordering} of candidates --- badges
re-rank the still-verifiable majority --- rather than by badge-driven
purchase decisions; synthetic-purchase counts do not rise with $s$, and
the agent's specification check, not the badge, remains the final filter
at decision time.

\textbf{(iii) A naive defense backfires.} Ranking candidates by their rating times the log of their review
count --- the most natural platform-side fix ---
is harmless on the clean Web (regret $0.256$) but \emph{amplifies} the
attack: success $0.300$, regret $0.507$ (vs.\ $0.443$ unprotected), and a
synthetic listing bought in $67.5\%$ of tasks. Manufactured social proof
hijacks the ranking signal: defenses built on the same surface features
the attacker optimizes are structurally complicit.

\textbf{(iv) Stealth pools (arms race).} The structural detector keys on
generator template fingerprints (superlatives, bullet-length symmetry,
\texttt{X:Y} spec lines, review boilerplate). Regenerating the pool with
fingerprint-free prompts (``stealth'' style: no marketing adjectives,
naturally varied bullets, prose-form specifications, humanlike reviews)
makes the attack itself slightly milder (unprotected regret $0.425$ vs.\
$0.443$ on the original pool) and partially degrades the detector: its
regret recovery falls from $0.193$ to $0.138$ --- a $28\%$ loss, though
the remaining recovery is still significant (paired Wilcoxon $p=0.022$).
The provenance badge is untouched by the stylistic shift: its recovery is
$0.152$ on the stealth pool versus $0.114$ on the original one, exactly
as expected for a defense that never reads the text
(Figure~\ref{fig:stress}c). The arms race
against content-based detection is thus real but gradual, while the
provenance channel is structurally immune to it.

\subsection{Backbone generality and scale}
\label{sec:res-backbone}
The dose--response replicates on Llama-3.1-8B
(success $0.475\to0.300$ at $\varepsilon=0.75$, $-17.5$\,pp; regret
$0.346\to0.502$, $+45\%$; synthetic purchases 55\%). Across scales,
smaller agents are \emph{at least} as susceptible --- at
$\varepsilon=0.75$, Qwen2.5-3B buys synthetic listings in 70\% of tasks
(vs.\ 54\% for 7B) and loses 27\% more regret --- but the success-level
dose contrast requires minimal baseline competence, which Qwen2.5-1.5B
lacks (success $0.300$ at $\varepsilon=0$, statistically unchanged under
contamination). The vulnerability is not an artifact of one model's
quirks. Summarizing the two competent backbones by a linear erosion
coefficient (regret increase per unit contamination, $\kappa$ from the
endpoint regression), Qwen2.5-7B gives $\kappa=0.242$ (95\% CI
$[0.129,0.352]$) and Llama-3.1-8B $\kappa=0.208$ (95\% CI
$[0.037,0.374]$) --- overlapping intervals, the same order of
vulnerability --- while the below-competence 1.5B backbone shows a
floor effect (clean regret already $0.578$) that makes the dose contrast
uninterpretable.

\subsection{The attack surface exists in real catalogs}
\label{sec:res-realworld}
The dose--response is measured on synthetic listings, so a natural
question is whether the tested discount regime matches reality. We
answer with three independent real-world sources, described in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-real}. First, in the human-verified ProMap
Walmart--Amazon mapping dataset~\cite{mackov2023promap}, 509
\emph{close non-match} pairs --- different products sharing brand,
product line, and near-identical titles, i.e., real-world analogues of
our clone channel --- list both prices in 330 cases; 73.3\% of these
have a side at least 10\% cheaper, with median discount 20.9\%.
Second, in WebMall~\cite{peeters2025webmall}, a benchmark of real
offers crawled from four shops in the October 2024 Common Crawl,
244 cross-shop groups of offers with \emph{identical normalized names}
(505 offers) show the same pattern: 53.3\% of groups have a side at
least 10\% cheaper (median 11.1\%) --- and for identical products,
buying the cheaper offer is the \emph{correct} decision, matching our
near-identical control condition. Third, blocking the 80{,}000-item
WebShop catalog sample~\cite{yao2022webshop} by brand and category and
matching title tokens (Jaccard $0.80$--$0.95$, distinct ASINs) yields
497 lookalike pairs; 83.3\% contain a side at least 10\% cheaper
(median 21.0\%), while 232 near-identical pairs (Jaccard
$\geq0.95$) sit at 51.7\% and 11.0\%. Across sources, the
$10\%$--$30\%$ discount range scanned in
Section~\ref{sec:res-dose} is not a tail event but the modal regime of
real price dispersion between lookalike offers, and the separation
between lookalike and near-identical dispersion is consistent across
all three sources. What the laboratory experiment adds is the causal
direction: real catalogs contain the lure structure, and
Section~\ref{sec:res-probe}--\ref{sec:res-dose} show that structure is
sufficient to buy agent decisions.

The mechanism also replicates with documents we did not generate. From
the full 1.18M-item catalog we mine 467 lookalike pairs (Appendix
\ref{sec:app-real}) whose pricier member carries an official human
instruction; the cheaper member --- a real listing, never synthesized
--- is the clone. In a forced-choice probe ($n{=}250$ per arm), agents
pick the \emph{real} lookalike in $61.6\%$ of episodes at
$f{=}0.9$ ($p{=}2.4\times10^{-4}$), $63.6\%$ at $f{=}0.8$, and
$60.0\%$ at its own unmodified price ($p{=}1.6\times10^{-3}$); on the
same tasks our generator's synthetic clone wins $67.7\%$ ---
statistically indistinguishable from the real lookalike (paired
McNemar $p{=}0.167$). The dose--response is thus not an artifact of
our generator: real listings in the clone niche buy decisions at the
same rate.
