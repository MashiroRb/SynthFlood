\subsection{Forced-choice probe: clones win, but why?}
\label{sec:res-probe}
The forced-choice probe confronts each agent with one gold product and one
synthetic listing (300 randomized duels). Because each task contributes ten
duels sharing its gold product and pool, all probe intervals in this paper
are \emph{task-cluster} bootstrap CIs (30 clusters); McNemar tests on
matched duels are retained as the pre-registered duel-level tests
(\S\ref{app:power}). Agents overall still prefer the
real product, $\sigma=0.377$ (cluster CI $[0.263,0.493]$; cluster-robust
vs.\ $\tfrac12$, $p=0.036$), but the families separate sharply:
$\sigma_{\text{clone}}=0.523$ (cluster CI $[0.366,0.688]$) ---
statistically indistinguishable from chance --- while
$\sigma_{\text{geo}}=0.351$ $[0.206,0.495]$ and
$\sigma_{\text{reviews}}=0.287$ $[0.156,0.441]$. Taken alone, this pattern invites a
perceptual reading: specification clones seem to pass as real products.
Trajectory inspection is consistent with such a reading, but
trajectories cannot separate what the agent \emph{fails to
see} from what it \emph{prefers to buy}; the next subsection closes that
gap with controlled channel ablations --- and reaches the opposite
conclusion.

\subsection{Channel decomposition: preference, not perception}
\label{sec:res-mechanism}
We re-run the clone duels under three controlled ablations of the synthetic
listing: \textbf{price parity} (clone priced exactly at the gold price),
\textbf{rating parity} (rating and review count reset to the gold listing's
genuine values), and \textbf{both} (each arm re-runs the same 300-duel
battery with identical tasks and pair seeds; the $n{=}88$ clone duels,
spanning 27 of the 30 task clusters, carry the comparison).
Either channel alone drops the clone win-rate from $0.523$ to $0.386$
(price parity: $\Delta\sigma=+0.136$, cluster CI $[-0.023,+0.300]$,
paired McNemar $+9/-21$, $p=0.043$; rating parity: $\Delta\sigma=+0.136$,
CI $[+0.028,+0.268]$, $+5/-17$, $p=0.017$); removing both drops it to
$0.250$ (cluster CI $[0.136,0.374]$; $\Delta\sigma=+0.273$, CI
$[+0.176,+0.390]$; paired $+3/-27$, $p<10^{-4}$). The two
single-channel contrasts are not equally reliable at the task level ---
price parity is directional only ($p=0.097$) --- so the conclusion rests
on the full-parity contrast and on the independent threshold law below,
read as a convergent joint pattern (\S\ref{app:power}).
The decomposition is additive to within half a point:
$0.523-0.137$ (price) $-0.136$ (social proof) $=0.250$ (Figure~\ref{fig:mech}a).
Two conclusions
follow. First, the clone's advantage is \emph{bought} with a discount and
manufactured ratings, in equal shares --- not earned by perceptual
similarity: at full price and honest ratings the clone is rejected three
times as often as chosen. Second, the residual $0.250$ falls to the
plain-gloss level ($\sigma_{\text{geo}}=0.351$, CI $[0.206,0.495]$, the
intervals overlapping): once the economic lures are removed, near-copying
the gold specifications stops helping the
attacker --- plausibly because close-but-altered specification text
makes the mismatch easier to notice.

\paragraph{A threshold law for discount pricing.}
Because price is the dominant channel, we dose it directly: the clone is
priced at a fixed fraction $f\in\{1.0,0.9,0.8,0.7\}$ of the gold price
with ratings left inflated ($n{=}88$ clone duels per level; paired against
the $f=1.0$ arm). The response is a \emph{threshold}, not a gradient: at price
parity agents reject the clone ($\sigma=0.386$), at $-10\%$ they already
prefer it ($\sigma=0.591$; paired $+26/-8$, duel-level McNemar $p=0.003$;
the parity-to-discount gain is cluster-robust, $+0.205$, 95\% CI
$[+0.050,+0.365]$, $p=0.011$),
and deeper discounts add nothing ($0.534$ at $-20\%$; $0.568$ at $-30\%$;
both within noise of the $-10\%$ point: cluster-robust $p=0.15$ and
$0.47$) --- all discount levels sit within noise of the original
mixed-discount pool ($0.523$). Figure~\ref{fig:mech}b plots the response.
In deployment terms, \emph{any} meaningful discount suffices; the
agent's price sensitivity saturates immediately past the
threshold.

\subsection{Dose--response: regret outruns success}
\label{sec:res-dose}
Table~\ref{tab:dose} gives the main curve over the
effective dose grid $\varepsilon\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75\}$ (with $N{=}10$
candidates, the nominal $0.1$ dose maps to zero replaced slots and is
omitted; each dose corresponds to 0, 2, 4, or 6 replaced slots),
$N{=}100$ tasks per dose under the Qwen2.5-7B backbone, plus a full
second-seed replication of the endpoints ($N{=}200$ paired observations).
Three co-movements define the silent-erosion signature.
\textbf{Success falls at the first dose step and stays depressed}: from $0.590$ at $\varepsilon=0$ to
$0.360$ at $\varepsilon=0.75$ (McNemar on paired tasks: 28 vs.\ 5
discordant, $\chi^2=14.7$, $p=1.3\times10^{-4}$; pooling both seeds, 64
vs.\ 13, $\chi^2=32.5$, $p=1.2\times10^{-8}$).
\textbf{Regret climbs even faster}: mean regret $0.262\to0.443$ ($+69\%$),
paired endpoint difference $+0.181$ (bootstrap 95\% CI $[+0.095,+0.264]$,
$t=4.2$). \textbf{Confidence rises throughout}: post-hoc verbalized
confidence increases from $0.822$ to $0.875$ while expected calibration
error \emph{doubles} ($0.249\to0.496$) --- agents err more while trusting
themselves more. The intermediate non-monotonicity of the curve (regret
$0.429\to0.401$ from $\varepsilon{=}0.25$ to $0.5$) is within paired
task-level noise (paired regret difference CI $[-0.102,+0.044]$; success
rebound $+0.070$, CI $[-0.020,+0.160]$).
The purchase share tells the mechanism directly: synthetic
listings are bought in 53\%, 42\%, and 54\% of tasks at
$\varepsilon=0.25,0.5,0.75$ (from 0\% at $\varepsilon=0$;
first-step paired contrast: 53 discordant tasks all one
direction, sign test $p<10^{-15}$; Cochran--Armitage trend over the four
doses $z=6.98$, one-sided $p=1.4\times10^{-12}$) --- a jump-and-plateau:
the first dose step carries the effect, and further contamination does
not deepen it. Purchases of
synthetic listings carry mean reward $0.35$ versus $0.80$ for purchases of
real listings at the same dose (both contamination seeds pooled).

\subsection{Family attribution}
\label{sec:res-family}
Single-family runs at $\varepsilon=0.75$ attribute the damage
(Table~\ref{tab:family} in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-family}): clones are
the most destructive at the success level (success $0.600\to0.425$,
regret $+41\%$; synthetic bought in $52.5\%$ of tasks), consistent with
the probe's decomposition; review-inflated pages deliver the largest
regret shock ($0.294\to0.454$, $+54\%$) --- a plain page with a
manufactured five-star flood gets purchased ($37.5\%$) because it looks
credible, then fails on missing specifications; geo is the mildest and
directional only (success flat; pooled paired regret difference $+0.075$,
CI $[-0.050,+0.195]$ crossing zero), though a purchased geo page earns
mean reward $0.22$ (versus $0.985$ for gold) --- a near-total loss.

\subsection{Defenses: provenance beats detection}
\label{sec:res-defense}
Table~\ref{tab:defense} evaluates the defense battery at
$\varepsilon=0.75$ against the paired uncontaminated baseline (same tasks,
same seeds; the \texttt{none} row is the unprotected high-dose condition).
Provenance re-ranking with an agent-visible \texttt{[verified]} badge ---
computed from a \emph{noisy} provenance bit (85\% coverage on real items,
15\% spurious on synthetic ones) --- recovers the regret excess in point
estimate
(0.398$\to$0.329 vs.\ the uncontaminated 0.338; marginal after
family-wise correction, \S\ref{app:power})
and cuts synthetic
purchases from 47.5\% to 32.5\%; the oracle badge (perfect provenance)
restores success \emph{above} the uncontaminated level (0.750 vs.\ 0.525)
and pushes regret below it (0.171), because knowing which listings are
verified makes agents scrutinize specifications harder. The structural
slop filter (marketing-cue density, keyword stuffing, bullet symmetry)
also fully recovers (regret 0.250, success 0.575): the generator families
leak templated structure even when their text is organic. Detection-style
defenses, by contrast, are brittle in deployment: the perplexity filter,
calibrated to near-perfect separation on held-out listings, collapses at
high dose (success 0.200, \emph{below} the unprotected 0.450) because its
hard threshold drops the true product --- whose generic text looks
``predictable'' --- while a tail of synthetic pages survives. The matched
random filter confirms the mechanism: dropping 30\% of candidates uniformly
\emph{improves} the clean baseline ($0.172$ vs.\ $0.338$) yet is no
better than no defense under contamination ($0.431$ vs.\ $0.398$) ---
filtering \emph{amount} is not filtering \emph{quality}. The prompt
defense achieves an intermediate 57\% regret recovery but changes
clean-Web behavior as well (a ``skepticism tax''), so its net value is
smaller than its high-dose delta.

Beyond the battery, four targeted experiments pin down \emph{how}
provenance information should be consumed and how robust the defenses are
(all at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$, 40 paired tasks, seed-1 pools unless noted).

\textbf{(i) Order, don't score.} Re-implementing the provenance bit as a
soft bonus $\lambda\cdot\mathbb{1}[\text{verified}]$ added to the platform
score fails for every weight within the rating scale (regret
$0.36$--$0.51$ for all $\lambda\leq6$) and only recovers at $\lambda{=}8$
($0.332$, statistically tied with the hard re-ranker's $0.329$). A bonus
smaller than the typical gap
between a manufactured 4.9-star rating and a genuine one is simply outbid:
provenance must act as a \emph{lexicographic} ordering constraint, not an
additive score.

\textbf{(ii) Forgery envelope.} Letting the adversary forge the badge on
$s\%$ of synthetic listings leaves recovery unchanged across the envelope:
regret $0.313$ ($s{=}35$), $0.332$ ($50$), $0.325$ ($65$) --- all
statistically indistinguishable from the no-forgery $0.329$ (Wilcoxon
$p\geq0.96$). The lone $s{=}25\%$ point scores
\emph{better} than baseline ($0.222$, $\Delta=-0.107$, $p=0.012$); five
trajectory replays attribute
the gap to greedy-chain behavioral variance, and
one favorable outlier among four rates is consistent with
multiplicity. Recovery is driven by the \emph{ordering} of candidates ---
badges re-rank the still-verifiable majority --- rather than by
badge-driven purchases: synthetic-purchase counts do not rise with $s$,
and the agent's specification check, not the badge, remains the final
filter at decision time.

\textbf{(iii) A naive defense backfires.} Ranking by
rating$\times\log$(review count) --- the most natural platform-side fix ---
is harmless on the clean Web (regret $0.256$) but \emph{amplifies} the
attack: success $0.300$, regret $0.507$ (vs.\ $0.443$ unprotected),
synthetic bought in $67.5\%$ of tasks. Manufactured social proof
hijacks the ranking signal: defenses built on the features the attacker
optimizes are structurally complicit.

\textbf{(iv) Stealth pools (arms race).} The structural detector keys on
generator template fingerprints. Regenerating the pool with
fingerprint-free prompts (``stealth'' style: no marketing adjectives,
naturally varied bullets, prose-form specifications, humanlike reviews)
leaves the unprotected level statistically unchanged on the same 40 tasks
(regret $0.425$ vs.\ $0.398$) and does
\emph{not} measurably degrade either defense at this sample size: the
structural filter's regret recovery is $0.139$ on the stealth pool versus
$0.148$ on the original one (paired Wilcoxon against the stealth
unprotected arm, $p=0.022$), and the provenance badge --- which never
reads text --- is unaffected by construction (recovery $0.153$ on the
stealth pool vs.\ $0.069$ on the original one). We therefore find no
detectable first-step arms-race erosion at $n{=}40$; the structural
detector's reliance on generator fingerprints remains a design concern,
and larger-sample replication is needed before any robustness claim.

\paragraph{Backbone generality.}
The dose--response also replicates on Llama-3.1-8B and across scales:
smaller competent agents are \emph{at least} as susceptible, and a
linear erosion coefficient $\kappa$ (regret increase per unit
contamination) is statistically indistinguishable across the two
competent 8B-class backbones ($0.242$ vs.\ $0.208$, overlapping CIs;
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-backbone}).

\subsection{Real catalogs, real documents}
\label{sec:res-realworld}
The tested discount regime matches reality, and the mechanism replicates
with documents we did not generate (pairing rules and per-source numbers
in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-real}). Across three sources, the tested
$10\%$--$30\%$ discounts are the modal regime of real price dispersion:
ProMap close non-matches~\cite{mackov2023promap}, 73.3\% of 330 pairs
with a side $\geq10\%$ cheaper (median 20.9\%); WebMall cross-shop
groups~\cite{peeters2025webmall}, 53.3\% of 244 (median 11.1\%) --- where
buying the cheaper offer is \emph{correct}, matching our near-identical
control; WebShop lookalike pairs~\cite{yao2022webshop}, 83.3\% of 497
(median 21.0\%). In a forced-choice probe over 467 real lookalike pairs
mined from the full catalog ($n{=}250$ per arm), agents pick the
\emph{real} lookalike in $61.6\%$ of episodes at $f{=}0.9$
($p{=}2.4\times10^{-4}$) and $60.0\%$ at its own unmodified price
($p{=}1.6\times10^{-3}$) --- statistically indistinguishable from our
generator's synthetic clone ($67.7\%$; paired McNemar $p{=}0.167$; the
95\% CI $[-2.3,+14.5]$\,pp bounds any generator advantage at
${\approx}15$\,pp). Real spam reviews~\cite{rayana2015collective} on
same-category listings preserve the direction of the harm (regret
$0.327$ vs.\ $0.294$ clean) but not its significance (21 non-tied pairs,
$p{=}1.0$), confounded with domain mismatch; the laboratory review
effect is an upper bound for attackers who control category-aligned
review text.

\subsection{Exposure leaves a transient memory trace}
\label{sec:res-memory}
A pre-registered washout experiment (\S\ref{app:m1})
asks whether contamination leaves anything behind: an agent browses
20 exposure episodes at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$, then shops \emph{only
clean pages}, taking forced-choice exams at clean episodes
$0,2,\ldots,20$ under four memory-ablation arms and a clean-experience
control (Table~\ref{tab:m1} in \S\ref{app:m1}). The fast channel is
real but transient: right after exposure the full-memory agent
prefers the discounted exam clone at $\sigma=0.897$ vs.\ $0.692$
pre-exposure (McNemar $p=0.021$); the excess decays with half-life
${\approx}4$ clean episodes and is statistically gone by episode 20
--- the pre-registered internalization test \emph{fails}, in seed-1
and under two pre-registered re-randomizations (in one the exam
instrument is byte-identical to seed-1). The carryover is carried by
read-back (write-only control at chance, $p=0.025$) and, across
seeds, reappears in direction ($+3$ to $+20$\,pp) but is significant
only in seed-1. The seed-1 price-parity elevation ($0.842$ vs.\
$0.579$, $p=0.011$) and its write-only mediation fall to noise under
both replications ($p{\ge}0.46$): we report both as single-seed
findings. Surgery and corrective feedback help only directionally.
All cells are single-model ($n{=}38$--$40$); the replication
protocol is pre-registered in \S\ref{app:m1} and released.
