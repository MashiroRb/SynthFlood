\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether the synthetic content common on the commercial Web
degrades agent decisions, and answered with a paired, dose-controlled
protocol on official artifacts. Agents are not fooled: channel
decomposition shows they reject clones once discounts and manufactured
ratings are removed; what fails is their \emph{preference alignment} ---
a ten-percent discount on a specification clone (ratings still
manufactured) restores majority-level clone preference ($0.591$ vs.\
$0.386$; cluster-robust $p=0.011$): content farms need not defeat the
agent's perception, only outbid it. Exposure also leaves a trace in
the agent's own written memory --- a preference bump that decays
within ten clean episodes and never internalizes across three
seeds. Regret jumps at the first dose and
stays elevated, while success metrics and confidence tell a reassuring
story. A single noisy provenance bit recovers most of the
regret excess --- but only consumed as an ordering constraint, a
semantics that should become a first-class Web--agent contract. We
release the SynthFlood protocol, pools, and trajectory logs to make
agent-Web robustness measurable, not anecdotal.

\section*{Ethics and Dual-Use Considerations}
This paper measures a harm, not provides a weapon: synthetic listings
are fabricated pseudo-products over WebShop's catalog IDs (used under
WebShop's license), released for defense research only. The exposed
mechanism is the standard content-farm playbook; our contribution is a
measurement protocol and defense principle, not an attack. No human
subjects; rollouts are simulated.
