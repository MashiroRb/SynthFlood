\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We asked whether the synthetic content already saturating the Web degrades
the decisions of the agents that browse it, and answered with a controlled,
paired, dose-controlled protocol on official benchmark artifacts. Agents
are not naive, and --- our central revised finding --- they are not fooled
either: channel decomposition shows they reject clones once discounts and
manufactured ratings are removed. What fails is their \emph{preference
alignment}: on a specification clone, a mere ten-percent discount
(with manufactured ratings still in place) suffices to flip the majority
of decisions, so ambient content farms do
not need to defeat the agent's perception, only to outbid it. Consequently
regret inflates with contamination dose while success metrics and agent
confidence tell a reassuring story. A single noisy provenance bit recovers
the full loss --- but only when the interface consumes it as an ordering
constraint rather than a score, a semantics we argue should become a
first-class contract between the Web and the agents acting on it. We
release the SynthFlood protocol, contamination pools, and all trajectory
logs to make agent-Web robustness a measurable property rather than an
anecdote.

\section*{Ethics and Dual-Use Considerations}
This paper measures a harm; it does not provide a weapon. All synthetic
listings are fabricated pseudo-products over WebShop's catalog IDs: they
inherit no real brands, sellers, prices, or review histories, cannot be
published on any real marketplace, and the pools are released for
defense research and replication only. The dose--response and
decomposition results make ambient contamination \emph{more} legible, not
more potent --- the mechanism we expose (a 10\% discount plus inflated
ratings) is already the standard playbook of commercial content farms, and
our contribution is a measurement protocol and a defense principle
(provenance-ordered ranking), not a novel attack. We follow WebShop's
original data licensing; no human subjects are involved, and all agent
rollouts are simulated purchases.
