\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Preference, not perception.}
Our own first reading of the probe --- the one a trajectory skim naturally
produces --- was that agents ``cannot tell'' clones from real products. The controlled decomposition falsifies that
reading. At price parity the clone is \emph{rejected} ($\sigma=0.386$); at
rating parity it is rejected identically ($0.386$); at both, it is
rejected almost $3{:}1$ ($0.250$) --- and the two channels decompose
additively, each worth roughly $14$ percentage points. The dangerous form
of ambient synthetic content is therefore not the attention-grabbing page
nor the perceptually perfect forgery, but the \emph{economically
attractive counterfeit}: a listing that copies a real product's
specifications and undercuts it with a discount and manufactured social
proof. The pricing dose--response sharpens this into a threshold law ---
a $10\%$ discount flips the majority, deeper discounts add nothing ---
which means the attack requires no optimization of the forgery itself,
only access to the pricing surface. This recasts the threat model:
attacks target the agent's \emph{preference alignment} (what it trades
off), not its perception (what it can detect), and defenses that shore up
detection leave the actual channel open. It also explains why
detector-style defenses based on surface statistics can only partially
help: the clone's text statistics look organic because its text \emph{is}
organic, copied from a real product. The structural detector nevertheless
was statistically indistinguishable from the provenance oracle in our
seed-1 runs (a parity claim our $n{=}40$ design cannot sharpen to
equivalence; \S\ref{app:power}) --- and on
fingerprint-free ``stealth'' pools it retains $72\%$ of its recovery
($p=0.022$) while the provenance badge is unaffected --- but we caution
that this robustness rests on generator fingerprinting and should be
treated as arms-race-bounded.

\paragraph{The economics of partial failure.}
Task success did fall under contamination --- but it fell by less than the
economics deteriorated: at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$ success lost $23$ points
while regret rose $69\%$ in relative terms, because most contamination
losses hide \emph{inside} ``successful'' and near-successful sessions as
attribute mismatches and overpayment rather than as outright failures.
Success-only dashboards therefore systematically understate the damage; an
operator sees a modest conversion dip while the wasted spend, misfulfillment
costs, and refund load grow much faster. The confidence inflation compounds
this: agents err more while trusting themselves more, so downstream
automation (reordering, auto-purchase budgets) inherits correlated,
concealed losses. We argue this pattern --- regret outrunning success,
confidence rising alongside error --- should become the standard diagnostic
pair for evaluating agents on live-content distributions, in the same way
calibration curves preceded accuracy as the trustworthy signal for
classifiers under distribution shift.

\paragraph{Provenance as an interface contract.}
Our defense results argue for a specific division of labor --- and for a
specific \emph{interface semantics}. Content-level detection asks every
consumer to re-litigate every page's authenticity, and it fails exactly on
the content that matters (near-copies of real pages). Provenance-weighted
ranking instead asks the supply side for one bit --- verifiable origin ---
and lets the agent treat its absence as a prior, not a verdict. Our
$\lambda$-sweep adds a semantics-level finding: the bit must enter the
ranking as an \emph{ordering constraint}, not an additive bonus. Soft
integration fails for every weight within the rating scale ($\lambda\leq
4$) because manufactured 4.9-star ratings outbid it, and only recovers
once the provenance term dominates ($\lambda=8$); a naive
popularity-signal defense that consumes rating$\times$review-count ---
precisely the features the attacker manufactures --- actively amplifies
the attack. An interface contract that exposes provenance should therefore
expose it as a sortable field, and platforms should treat
``provenance-blind'' ranking signals as attack surface. Because the bit is
cheap to spoof in the model but expensive to spoof in the world (a
signature over seller identity and listing history), it converts an
unbounded text-classification arms race into a bounded key-management
problem --- and our forgery envelope (unchanged through $65\%$ badge
forgery) shows the noisy-bit design tolerates adversarial display
spoofing. The noise we inject (15\% missing on real items, 15\% present on
synthetic ones) is an intentionally pessimistic model of real-world
deployment, and recovery remains high; the remaining gap to the oracle is
the price of noisy provenance, not of the idea.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our agents are 7--8B open-weight models with a scripted action interface;
frontier models with stronger browsing may behave differently --- though
the channel decomposition suggests the vulnerability lives in preference
alignment rather than perceptual limits, so capability alone may not close
it; the erosion coefficient is statistically indistinguishable across our
two 8B-class backbones ($0.242$ vs.\ $0.208$, overlapping CIs). The
mechanism decomposition and the pricing threshold law are measured on one
backbone (Qwen2.5-7B) with one generator pair; both are pre-registered
comparisons released with the paper, and we flag cross-generator
replication as the natural next test. The contamination families are
generated by models of the same size class as the agents; industrial
content farms use larger generators, which could tune prices and ratings
more aggressively, but our threshold law suggests any discount at all
suffices. WebShop instructions are variant-level and sometimes weakly aligned
with the base listing text; official best-variant scoring handles
this, making absolute success numbers benchmark-specific without
interacting with the within-task randomized contamination. The
dose--response uses a single item pool per task; pools are fixed
across $\varepsilon$ (paired design), defense arms are replicated on
an independent second-generation pool, and all pools and seeds are
released. ``Ambient'' is read as how content arrives --- present in
the routine retrieval context, without targeting --- not whole-web
saturation; the real-document experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:res-realworld}) close the self-generation gap for
the clone channel, narrow it for reviews (directionally consistent,
not significant under domain mismatch), and the fifty-slot
experiment localizes the strong harm to contamination density. Two secondary
findings are reported at reduced strength after replication: pooled
over two pools, the geo-family regret excess is directional only (CI
crosses zero), and the single favorable point in the forgery envelope
($s{=}25\%$) is adjudicated as behavioral variance. Provenance
availability in the wild is an empirical question our 85/15 noise
model approximates crudely; measuring real provenance coverage on
major marketplaces is a natural next step.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
If agentic browsing becomes a dominant traffic source, the incentive to
optimize \emph{for agents} will exceed the incentive to optimize for human
attention, because agents can be measured and A/B-tested at scale. The
question is not whether the Web's supply side adapts to machine readers ---
it already is --- but whether machine readers will be able to tell what
adapted. Ambient contamination is the benign end of that adaptation spectrum;
deliberate manipulation is the malign end, and both flow through the same
missing infrastructure: content whose origin a buyer can verify.
