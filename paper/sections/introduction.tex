\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Web agents have crossed from demos to deployment: they shop, book, and
compare on our behalf, turning the Web's product pages from artifacts that
\emph{inform humans} into signals that \emph{direct machine decisions}. At
the same time, the supply side of those pages is being rewritten. An
estimated 27.5\% of tokens passing common quality filters on the open Web
are now AI-generated, and the share is climbing monthly \cite{russell2026much};
measurement studies document machine-generated catalogs flooding music
platforms \cite{wu2026empirical} and scientific publishing \cite{oh2026science}.
This content is not adversarial in the classical sense: it is produced
by ordinary commercial incentives --- search-optimized content farms,
counterfeit-style listing clones, review inflation --- and a research
program now studies how to \emph{optimize} content for generative engines
\cite{wu2025what,bagga2025egeo,nimase2026geobench} and how to detect the
result \cite{chu2026geoflag,zheng2026countergeobench,li2026when}. The
consequence is that agentic systems increasingly browse a Web
whose supply of plausible, keyword-rich, well-rated listings no longer
correlates with actually satisfying the user's goal.

This paper asks a question that, to our knowledge, no prior work has measured
in a controlled setting: \textbf{does ambient, non-targeted synthetic content
silently erode the decision quality of web agents?} Existing evaluations of
agents under Web pollution attack them deliberately: adversarially curated
mini-internets \cite{shah2026synthetic}, attacker-written
endorsement pages steering search agents \cite{chen2026much}, prompt
injection and memory poisoning \cite{cao2026ward,yang2026zombie,syros2026muzzle};
upstream of the decision, cumulative AI pollution degrades retrieval quality
\cite{yu2026retrieval}. Ambient contamination is a different regime: no
adversary knows the user's goal, so no page is crafted to defeat a specific
decision --- the threat is statistical, not surgical, and whether it
materially matters for agent \emph{economics} (choice quality, wasted
spend, calibrated confidence) is unknown.

Measuring this regime requires disentangling choice quality from task
difficulty: a naive before/after comparison cannot tell whether agents
chose worse or the tasks became harder. We therefore build
\textbf{SynthFlood}, a paired, dose-controlled protocol on WebShop's
official artifacts~\cite{yao2022webshop}: each task renders a fixed
candidate list in which the gold product is always present and never
replaced; only distractor slots are progressively replaced by
synthetic listings, drawn under a fixed seed so lower doses are nested
within higher ones. Synthetic listings come from a three-family
generator that mirrors real content-farm modes --- search-optimized
pages, specification clones with lower prices, and review-inflated
pages --- with a cross-model plausibility floor, all generated by
open-weight 7--8B models. Agents act in a ReAct loop over rendered
listings; every purchase is scored by WebShop's \emph{official} reward
function, so regret is measured in the benchmark's own currency.
Figure~\ref{fig:teaser} previews the threat and the four findings that
organize the paper.

SynthFlood yields four measurements: a \emph{forced-choice
susceptibility probe} (gold product vs.\ one synthetic listing, 300
randomized duels, yielding $\sigma$ --- the probability of buying the
machine-optimized page over the correct product); a
\emph{dose--response curve} over $\varepsilon\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75\}$
with McNemar tests on paired tasks, where our silent-erosion
hypothesis predicts regret rising steeply while coarse success appears
stable (partial mismatches are invisible in success counts); a
\emph{defense battery} at matched contamination --- its critical
control drops the same number of candidates uniformly, and its
headline defense re-ranks by a noisy verifiable provenance bit without
ever seeing labels; and a \emph{washout experiment} that returns
exposed agents to clean shopping to test whether contamination leaves
a memory-carried residue.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A threat model and protocol} for ambient (non-targeted)
      synthetic contamination of agent decision channels, with paired
      dose--response control on official benchmark artifacts.
\item \textbf{A forced-choice probe with channel decomposition}
      establishing \emph{why} clones win: price parity and rating parity
      each cut the clone win-rate from $0.523$ to $0.386$, and removing
      both drops it to $0.250$ --- an additive decomposition attributing
      the attack to price and social proof, not perception. A pricing
      dose--response yields a threshold law: a 10\% discount restores
      majority-level clone preference ($0.591$ vs.\ $0.386$ at parity;
      cluster-robust $p=0.011$), with no further gain at deeper
      discounts.
\item \textbf{To our knowledge, the first decision-level
      dose--response evidence} that ambient
      contamination erodes agent economics: at $\varepsilon=0.75$, success
      drops by $23$\,pp ($p=1.2\times10^{-8}$ pooled over two
      seed-replicated pools) and regret rises $69\%$ (paired
      $\Delta=+0.181$, 95\% CI $[+0.095,+0.264]$), while confidence rises
      and calibration error doubles --- errors that no success metric
      would surface.
\item \textbf{A defense analysis with an ordering principle}: an
      agent-visible noisy provenance badge recovers the regret excess in
      point estimate (marginal after family-wise correction;
      \S\ref{app:power}) and is unchanged under badge forgery up to
      $65\%$; a text-free structural filter drives regret below the
      uncontaminated level (indistinguishable from a perfect-provenance
      oracle at this sample size); but consuming the same bit as a soft
      ranking bonus fails until its weight exceeds the rating scale ---
      and a naive popularity ranking \emph{backfires}, raising synthetic
      purchases to 67.5\%.
\item \textbf{Real-document external validation}: real lookalike
      listings from a 1.18M-item catalog reproduce the clone effect
      ($\sigma=0.60$--$0.64$; the paired difference to our generator's
      clone is bounded by 95\% CI $[-2.3,+14.5]$\,pp), and Yelp
      fraud-filtered spam reviews reproduce the review channel's
      direction (not its significance).
\item \textbf{Exposure leaves a memory-carried residue}: after twenty
      contaminated episodes, a memory-equipped agent treats a
      \emph{full-price} lookalike the way a naive agent treats a
      discounted one (parity exam $\sigma=0.842$ vs.\ $0.579$,
      $p=0.011$); the residue is absent without memory read-back
      ($0.526$), forms despite consistent punishment, and does not
      transfer even within the exposure category ($p=0.80$).
\end{itemize}
An anonymized package with code, contamination pools, and trajectory
logs accompanies the submission; the remainder of the paper formalizes
the threat model (\S\ref{sec:problem}), presents the protocol
(\S\ref{sec:method}), reports results (\S\ref{sec:results}), discusses
implications and limitations (\S\ref{sec:discussion}), and concludes
(\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).
