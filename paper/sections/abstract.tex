Web agents buy on our behalf on a Web where a quarter of the text is
machine-generated under commercial optimization pressure; unlike
adversarial injection, this \emph{ambient} contamination targets no one
and merely occupies the routine retrieval context, yet it may distort
what agents choose. We present \textbf{SynthFlood}, a paired,
dose-controlled protocol measuring how synthetic listings erode
web-agent decision quality on WebShop's official data and reward, with
three contamination families mirroring real content farms. Four
findings. (1)~In a forced-choice probe agents buy specification
clones at chance (52\%), but the attack buys their \emph{preference},
not their perception: removing the discount or inflated ratings alone
drops its win-rate to 39\% (both, to 25\%), and a 10\% discount
restores majority-level preference (59\% vs.\ 39\%; cluster-robust
$p{=}0.011$). (2)~Contamination degrades economics faster than success
metrics reveal: at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$ success drops 23\,pp (pooled
$p{=}1.2\times10^{-8}$), regret rises $69\%$, calibration error
doubles. (3)~A noisy provenance badge
recovers the regret excess in point estimate (marginal after
family-wise correction) and tolerates 65\% badge forgery---but only as
an \emph{ordering constraint}: as a soft bonus it fails until its
weight exceeds the rating scale. (4)~Exposure leaves a transient,
read-back-carried bump (McNemar $p{=}0.021$) that never internalizes
across three seeds; a parity-level residue appears in one seed only. The effect replicates
with \emph{real} documents: lookalikes from a 1.18M-item catalog
are bought at rates indistinguishable from our synthetic clones (95\%
CI $[-2.3,+14.5]$\,pp bounds any generator advantage). These results
recast ambient synthetic content as an attack on the agent's
\emph{preference function} and motivate provenance-ordered ranking as
an interface contract between Web and agents.
