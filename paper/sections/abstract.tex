Web agents already shop and buy on our behalf, on a Web whose
commercial supply is increasingly machine-written under ordinary
optimization pressure. Unlike adversarial injection, this
\emph{ambient} contamination targets no one and merely occupies the
routine retrieval context --- yet autonomous agents browse, compare,
and buy amid it. We present \textbf{SynthFlood}, a paired,
dose-controlled protocol measuring how synthetic listings erode
web-agent decision quality on WebShop's official data and reward, with
three contamination families mirroring real content farms. Four
findings. (1)~In a forced-choice probe agents buy specification
clones at chance (52\%), but the attack buys their \emph{preference},
not their perception: removing the discount or inflated ratings alone
drops the clone win-rate to 39\% (both, to 25\%), and a 10\% discount
restores majority-level preference (59\% vs.\ 39\%; cluster-robust
$p{=}0.011$). (2)~Contamination degrades economics faster than success
metrics reveal: at $\varepsilon{=}0.75$ success drops 23\,pp (pooled
$p{=}1.2\times10^{-8}$), regret rises $69\%$, calibration error
doubles. (3)~A single noisy provenance bit recovers the regret excess
in point estimate (marginal after family-wise correction) and
tolerates 65\% badge forgery --- but only consumed as an
\emph{ordering constraint} (confirmed at $N{=}100$ paired tasks); the
same bit as a soft ranking bonus fails. (4)~Exposure leaves a
transient, read-back-carried preference bump (McNemar $p{=}0.021$)
that never internalizes across three seeds; a parity-level residue
appears in one seed only. With \emph{real} documents, lookalikes from
a 1.18M-item catalog are bought at rates indistinguishable from our
synthetic clones (95\% CI $[-2.3,+14.5]\,pp$ bounds any generator
advantage). These results recast ambient synthetic content as an
attack on the agent's \emph{preference function} and motivate
provenance-ordered ranking as an interface contract between Web and
agents.
