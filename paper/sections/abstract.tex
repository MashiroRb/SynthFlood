Web agents increasingly buy on our behalf, while roughly a quarter of the
text they read is machine-generated under commercial optimization pressure.
Unlike adversarial injection attacks, this \emph{ambient} contamination
targets no one --- yet it may systematically distort what agents choose. We
present \textbf{SynthFlood}, a paired, dose-controlled protocol that
measures how synthetic product listings erode web-agent decision quality,
built on WebShop's official data and reward function. Synthetic listings
mirror three real content-farm modes (search-optimized pages,
specification clones with altered attributes and lower prices,
review-inflated pages), generated by open-weight 7--8B models under a
cross-model plausibility floor. Three findings emerge. First, a
forced-choice probe shows agents buy near-correct specification clones at
chance (52\%), but controlled ablations show this is \emph{not} a
perceptual failure: normalizing the clone's price or stripping its
inflated ratings alone each drop its win-rate to 39\%, and removing both
drops it to 25\% --- below the 35\% baseline for plain gloss. The same
clone is rejected at price parity yet \emph{preferred} under any discount
of 10\% or more (59\%, $p=0.003$): the attack buys the agent's
\emph{preference}, not its perception. Second, contamination degrades
decisions faster than success metrics reveal: at $\varepsilon=0.75$,
success drops 23\,pp ($p=1.2\times10^{-8}$ pooled over two seed-replicated
pools) while regret rises 69\% and agents become \emph{more} confident
(calibration error doubles). Third, an agent-visible badge computed from a
noisy verifiable-provenance bit eliminates the regret excess and tolerates
badge forgery up to 65\% --- but only when consumed as an ordering
constraint; the same bit added as a soft ranking bonus fails until its
weight exceeds the rating scale. An anonymized package with protocol,
code, contamination pools, and full trajectory logs accompanies the
submission. These results recast ambient synthetic content as an attack on
the agent's preference function and motivate provenance-ordered ranking as
an interface contract between the Web and the agents that act on it.
